It starts with a few words. "Hi, is this Sarah?" Wrong number. You almost ignore it. Almost.

In twenty twenty-five, Americans reported losing nearly twenty-one billion dollars to online crime. The single biggest category? Crypto investment scams. Seven point two billion dollars.

One of those messages reached the CEO of a small-town bank in Kansas. Within months, he had wired away forty-seven million dollars. His bank collapsed. Today he's serving twenty-four years in federal prison.

And the people typing on the other end? Many of them were prisoners too. Trafficked, locked inside compounds on the other side of the world, and forced to type.

This is the story of pig butchering. The scam that fattens you up... before it takes everything. And by the end of this video, you'll know the exact moment to stop replying.

Chapter one. Wrong number.

It usually begins with a stranger. A text. A message on Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp, LinkedIn, or a dating app. You reply: sorry, wrong number. They apologize... and keep talking.

They're friendly. Successful. Good-looking. Their photos show sports cars, rooftop dinners, first-class travel. They never ask for money. Not at first.

Instead they ask about your day. Your job. Your divorce. Your debts. Every day, for weeks.

The scammers have a name for this playbook. In Chinese, it's called sha zhu pan. The pig-butchering plate. First, you fatten the pig. Then, you slaughter it.

Chapter two. The fattening.

Once they have your trust, the stranger lets you in on a secret. An uncle who trades crypto. A platform with steady returns. They'll teach you, step by step.

The app looks real. Live charts. Price tickers. A customer support chat. You start small. A few hundred dollars. The next day, your balance is up.

A small business owner in Maryland, whom CNBC called Dennis, was messaged by a woman named "Sarah." She said she was a wealthy executive. He was going through a separation. She talked with him for hours, every single day.

On the screen, his trades kept winning. Around twenty percent at a time. His balance climbed past one point two million dollars. Then he tried to take it out.

Chapter three. The slaughter.

The account froze. To unlock it, he was told to pay a hundred and eighty thousand dollars.

That's the moment the whole scam is built for. A tax. A fee. A penalty. Pay a little more, and you'll get it all back.

None of the money was ever there. The platform was fake. The profits were just numbers on a screen. Dennis lost around half a million dollars.

And then came the cruelest part. A "recovery firm" contacted him, promising to get his money back. For a fee. It was the same kind of scam. And he lost even more.

A teacher in Hawaii, whom CNBC called Kaimi, met a "handsome stranger" on Instagram. Months later, he had lost more than a hundred and twenty thousand dollars, much of it paying "fees" to free his own money.

Chapter four. The banker.

You might be thinking: that would never happen to me. Shan Hanes probably thought that too.

He was the CEO of Heartland Tri-State Bank in Elkhart, Kansas. A town of about two thousand people. A banker. A pillar of the community.

In late twenty twenty-two, he was pulled into a crypto scam that started on WhatsApp. First, he used his own money. Then, according to prosecutors, he took forty thousand dollars from his church. Ten thousand from an investment club. Sixty thousand from his daughter's college fund.

Then he started wiring the bank's money. Eleven wires in about eight weeks. One for one point five million dollars. One for ten million.

Forty-seven million dollars. Gone. He never made a single cent.

In July twenty twenty-three, regulators shut the bank down. Depositors were protected. Shareholders were wiped out. In August twenty twenty-four, a federal judge sentenced him to more than twenty-four years in prison. Longer than prosecutors had asked for.

Chapter five. Who's really typing.

So who is on the other end of those messages? Often, not a con artist in a mansion. A young person in a guarded compound, who answered a job ad for customer service or tech work... and was driven across a border.

Passports taken. Phones taken. Daily quotas. Former workers have described seventeen-hour days, beatings, and threats against anyone who tried to escape. The only way out was often a ransom they called a "contract termination fee."

One of the most notorious compounds, K K Park, sits on the Myanmar-Thailand border. When Myanmar's military raided it in October twenty twenty-five, it said it found more than two thousand people inside... and more than thirty Starlink satellite internet terminals.

Satellite internet. Keeping the scam online. Keeping the messages flowing to phones in America.

Chapter six. The fifteen billion dollar seizure.

On October fourteenth, twenty twenty-five, federal prosecutors in Brooklyn unsealed charges against a man named Chen Zhi.

Prosecutors say he led the Prince Group, a Cambodian conglomerate that looked like a real-estate and finance empire, and ran forced-labor scam compounds behind it.

The Justice Department seized one hundred twenty-seven thousand bitcoin. About fifteen billion dollars at the time. Officials called it the largest forfeiture action in U.S. history. Prosecutors said that at its peak, the operation made up to thirty million dollars a day.

In January twenty twenty-six, Chen Zhi was arrested in Cambodia... and sent to China. Not to America. The U.S. case is stalled. And his lawyers are fighting to get the bitcoin back.

Chapter seven. The fight back.

Washington is fighting back. A new federal Scam Center Strike Force has seized more than five hundred eighty million dollars in crypto tied to these networks.

But for most victims, money that leaves their account never comes back. Which means the best defense... is knowing the playbook before it reaches you.

Chapter eight. The red flags.

So here are the red flags.

One. A stranger who "accidentally" messages you, and keeps chatting after you say wrong number.

Two. Someone you've never met in person who brings up crypto, forex, or a trading app that never loses.

Three. A platform that wants you to pay fees or taxes before you can withdraw your own money. Real exchanges don't work that way.

Four. Anyone who promises to recover your lost money for an upfront fee.

If it's happened to you or someone you love, report it to the FBI at I C 3 dot gov. And tell someone. Shame is exactly what these scammers count on.

Because right now, somewhere, someone is typing the next message. "Hi, is this Sarah?"
