Elon Musk has thousands of satellites circling the Earth. He can beam internet to a boat in the middle of the ocean. To a war zone. To a cabin in Alaska.

But there's one country he still can't get into. A country of one point four billion people.

And earlier this month, Musk went online and asked a strange question. Not to a president. Not to a prime minister. To a businessman. He called him... "Prime Minister Ambani."

So who is this man? And why does the richest person on Earth seem to think he's standing in the way?

Chapter one. The cheapest internet on Earth.

Right now, in America, a gigabyte of mobile data can cost you several dollars. In India, it costs about eight cents.

Eight cents. That's not a typo. It's among the cheapest mobile data anywhere in the world.

And it's cheap because of one man. Mukesh Ambani. The richest person in Asia. The chairman of Reliance Industries. A company that started out making polyester... and now refines oil, runs supermarkets, and owns India's biggest phone network.

That network is called Jio.

Chapter two. The free internet ambush.

In September twenty sixteen, Jio launched. And it did something no phone company had ever done at that scale.

It gave everything away. Free calls. Free data. For months.

Imagine a new carrier showing up in America and saying: unlimited everything, zero dollars. What would you do?

That's exactly what India did. Jio signed up one hundred million customers in just one hundred and seventy days. About seven new customers every single second.

For the old phone companies, it was a disaster. Their customers walked out the door. Their profits collapsed.

Some sold out. Some merged to survive. And some shut down completely. One of the companies that collapsed was run by Mukesh Ambani's own younger brother.

When the dust settled, data prices across India had crashed by more than ninety percent. Hundreds of millions of people got online for the first time.

And Jio was the king. Today it has more than five hundred million subscribers.

So remember this playbook. Go in cheap. Take the whole market. Then decide the price.

Because now, someone wants to run that same playbook... against Jio.

Chapter three. The rocket man knocks.

Starlink first tried to enter India back in twenty twenty-one. More than seven thousand Indians paid a ninety-nine dollar deposit to pre-order.

Then the government stepped in. Starlink didn't have a license. It was told to stop taking orders... and refund every single deposit.

So Starlink went back to the start. And the paperwork began.

In twenty twenty-four, a new fight broke out. Over the invisible part of satellite internet. The airwaves. Spectrum.

Reliance argued that satellite spectrum should be sold at auction. Like the airwaves its own phone network bought for billions.

Musk fired back on X. He called an auction "unprecedented."

And this time, Musk won. India's telecom minister announced the spectrum would be handed out administratively. No auction.

Round one, Elon.

Chapter four. The strange handshake.

Then, in March twenty twenty-five, something nobody expected.

Jio signed a deal with SpaceX. To sell Starlink equipment in its own stores.

Its biggest rival, Airtel, signed one too.

So the two giants of Indian telecom were now partners with the man who wanted to compete with them.

Why? One theory: keep your friends close... and your rivals closer. If Starlink was coming anyway, better to be the one selling it.

And by the summer of twenty twenty-five, Starlink reportedly had its license. Gateways were being built. It looked like launch day was close.

It wasn't.

Chapter five. Prime Minister Ambani.

Fast forward to October twenty twenty-six. Starlink says its ground stations in India are ready. But it still has zero paying customers there.

The last steps keep dragging on. Security clearances. Final spectrum pricing.

And Musk ran out of patience.

He posted on X, blaming Indian "oligarchs" for holding Starlink back. Then he went further. He addressed Mukesh Ambani as "Prime Minister Ambani"... and asked if he would allow Starlink to compete.

The message was hard to miss. Musk was suggesting that one businessman had more say than the government.

India's government pushed back hard. It said there's no favoritism. Starlink, Jio's own satellite venture, and a third company are all waiting on the same security checks.

Reliance didn't reply at all.

Then Musk made a promise. If Starlink is too expensive in India, he said, nobody will buy it. So the price will have to be affordable.

And he added one line aimed straight at Ambani. His profits will be lower... but he's not going out of business.

Chapter six. The empire strikes back.

Ambani didn't answer with a tweet. He answered with satellites.

A few days later, at India's biggest telecom show, Jio unveiled its own space plan. Nearly sixteen hundred satellites. Twenty-three ground stations. Built with the satellite company SES.

The target? More than six hundred thousand villages. The exact places Starlink wanted to win first.

And then came the money.

That same month, Jio Platforms opened India's largest stock market listing ever. It raised about three point two billion dollars, at a value of roughly one hundred and eight billion.

Big investors didn't flinch at the feud. Institutional buyers bid for more than twenty-six times the shares set aside for them.

So while Musk was posting, Ambani was raising money. And building a satellite network of his own.

Chapter seven. Who actually wins?

Here's the thing most people miss.

Starlink isn't really fighting Jio for the big cities. In Mumbai, Jio's fiber and five G are fast and dirt cheap. A satellite dish can't compete with eight cents a gigabyte.

The real battle is everywhere else. Remote villages. Mountains. Islands. Border regions. Places where no cable will ever reach.

That's where hundreds of millions of people are still offline.

And that's where the next fortune will be made.

Chapter eight. The playbook comes home.

So think back to twenty sixteen. A newcomer showed up with cheap prices and took over India's internet.

That newcomer was Jio.

Ten years later, the newcomer is Elon Musk. And he's promising the exact same thing. Cheap prices. Everyone connected.

The question isn't whether Musk can build the better network. It's whether Mukesh Ambani will let history repeat itself... against him.
