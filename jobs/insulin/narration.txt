In twenty seventeen, a young man in Minnesota named Alec Smith turned twenty-six.

On his birthday, he aged off his mother's health insurance.

Alec managed a restaurant. He made about thirty-five thousand dollars a year. Too much for government help. Not enough for a good plan.

He had type one diabetes. Without insurance, his pharmacist estimated his supplies would cost about thirteen hundred dollars a month.

Less than a month later, Alec was found alone in his apartment. He had died from diabetic ketoacidosis. Three days before payday. His family believes he had been rationing his insulin.

Here's the part that should make you angry. The people who discovered insulin sold the patent for one dollar.

So how did a one dollar medicine become something people die without?

Chapter one. The one dollar miracle.

Before nineteen twenty-two, type one diabetes was a death sentence. Doctors could only put children on starvation diets. It bought them a few months. Sometimes a year.

Then, in Toronto, a young surgeon named Frederick Banting and his team found a way to extract insulin.

In January nineteen twenty-two, they injected a fourteen-year-old boy named Leonard Thompson. He was dying. And he came back.

Within a year, insulin was being made in bulk. Children who had been given months to live were going home.

Banting could have become one of the richest men alive. Instead, the discoverers sold the patent to the University of Toronto. For one dollar.

Banting's reason was simple. Insulin does not belong to me, he said. It belongs to the world.

Chapter two. Three companies.

The university needed help to make insulin at scale. So it partnered with drug companies.

A hundred years later, about ninety percent of the world's insulin market is controlled by just three of them. Eli Lilly. Novo Nordisk. And Sanofi.

And over the decades, they kept improving the product. Purer insulin. Faster insulin. Longer-lasting insulin. Better pens.

Every improvement came with a new patent. And every new patent meant a new chance to set a new price.

Chapter three. The price climb.

In nineteen ninety-six, Eli Lilly launched a fast-acting insulin called Humalog. The list price was about twenty-one dollars a vial.

By twenty nineteen, that same vial had a list price of about two hundred and seventy-five dollars.

Same medicine. Same vial. More than thirteen times the price.

And it wasn't just one drug. Between twenty twelve and twenty sixteen, the price of insulin in America more than doubled.

When one company raised its price, the others often followed. A U.S. Senate investigation found that Novo Nordisk and Sanofi tracked each other's prices and raised them in lockstep. Sometimes within days.

But here's the strange part. The companies said they weren't actually keeping most of that money.

So where was it going?

Chapter four. The middlemen.

Between the drug company and your pharmacy, there's a player most Americans have never heard of. The pharmacy benefit manager. The PBM.

PBMs work for insurance plans. They decide which drugs are covered and which ones aren't. That list is called a formulary. And getting on it is worth billions.

So drug companies pay PBMs secret discounts, called rebates, to get on the list.

Here's the catch. The rebate is a percentage of the list price. So the higher the list price, the bigger the rebate. And the more the middleman makes.

The Senate report found rebates for insulin grew from single digits in twenty thirteen to sixty percent or more for the biggest buyers.

It even found that Novo Nordisk's board voted down a proposal to cut its insulin price. One of the reasons? Fear of backlash from the PBMs and insurers.

Think about that. A lower price was seen as a business risk.

Chapter five. Who pays the sticker price?

Now, insurance companies got those big rebates. But not everyone has a plan that passes the savings on.

If you're uninsured, you pay the list price. If you have a high deductible, you pay the list price until you hit it. Every January, that clock resets.

And that list price is wildly out of line with the rest of the world. A twenty twenty-four RAND study found that American list prices for insulin were nearly ten times higher than in thirty-three other wealthy countries combined.

So people did what Alec did. A Yale study found that about one in four diabetes patients had used less insulin than prescribed because of the cost.

They skipped doses. They stretched vials. Some crossed the border into Canada. And some, like Alec, didn't make it.

Chapter six. The crack in the wall.

Alec's mother, Nicole Smith-Holt, became one of the loudest voices in a growing movement. Parents. Patients. Doctors. All asking the same question. Why does this cost so much?

The pressure finally broke through.

In twenty twenty-two, Congress passed a law capping insulin at thirty-five dollars a month for people on Medicare.

Then, on March first, twenty twenty-three, Eli Lilly announced it would cut the list price of its most common insulins by seventy percent. And cap what many patients pay at thirty-five dollars a month.

Within weeks, Novo Nordisk and Sanofi announced cuts of their own.

And in January twenty twenty-six, California started selling its own state-branded insulin. Generic long-acting pens for about eleven dollars each.

After decades of climbing, the wall finally cracked.

Chapter seven. What it actually costs.

So how much does insulin really cost to make?

A study in the journal BMJ Global Health estimated it. For most modern insulins, about seventy-eight to one hundred and thirty-three dollars per person. Not per vial. For a whole year.

In many countries, governments cap the price by law. In India, basic human insulin is under a government price ceiling. A vial can cost just a few dollars.

Same science. Same hormone. Discovered by the same people who wanted it to belong to the world.

Chapter eight. The one dollar promise.

A hundred years ago, Frederick Banting gave away a fortune because he believed no one should die for lack of insulin.

In twenty seventeen, a twenty-six-year-old restaurant manager died three days before payday, with an empty insulin pen.

Prices are finally falling. But Alec's story is a reminder of what happens when a medicine becomes a business model.

The patent was sold for one dollar. The question is who decided what it should cost after that.
