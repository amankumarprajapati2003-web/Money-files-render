Right now, there's a number on your phone that decides what you can eat this week. Your bank balance. You check it. You worry about it. You work forty hours a week to make it go up.

But for almost all of human history, that number didn't exist. No coins. No banks. No bills. For hundreds of thousands of years, humans lived with no money at all.

So how did they get anything?

You probably learned the answer in school. They bartered. I'll give you three fish for your spear. Then barter got annoying, so someone invented money.

It's a great story. There's just one problem. It almost certainly never happened.

Chapter one. The barter myth.

Here's a test. Two hunters. One has meat. The other has a brand new spear. The first one wants the spear. But the second one isn't hungry. He wants a warm fur. So... what happens?

In the barter story, nothing. No deal. Everyone goes home angry. Economists even have a name for this problem: the double coincidence of wants.

The story comes from Adam Smith, in seventeen seventy-six. A baker has bread. A butcher has meat. But the butcher already has bread. So no exchange can be made.

Anthropologists went looking for these barter economies. All over the world. For more than a century.

In nineteen eighty-five, Cambridge anthropologist Caroline Humphrey summed up what they found. No example of a barter economy, pure and simple, has ever been described.

Not one. So if they didn't barter... what did they actually do?

Chapter two. The gift.

Picture a camp in the Kalahari, thousands of years ago. A hunter comes home with a big antelope.

He doesn't sell it. He doesn't trade it. He shares it. With everyone.

Why would anyone give away the most valuable thing they own? Because next week, he might come home with nothing. And then someone else shares with him.

Among the Zhu-twa-see people of southern Africa, anthropologist Polly Wiessner studied a gift system called kharo. People gave beads, arrows and tools to partners who lived far away. Sometimes they waited months, or years, for a gift in return.

That wasn't generosity. It was insurance. If drought hit your land, you could walk to your kharo partner's camp... and they had to take you in.

So the first "money" wasn't a thing. It was a relationship. A promise. I'll remember what you did for me.

Chapter three. The beads.

In a cave on the coast of South Africa, called Blombos Cave, archaeologists found something strange. Tiny sea shells, each with a hole in it. Strung together. Seventy-five thousand years old.

They're some of the oldest jewelry ever found. And jewelry is interesting. You can't eat it. You can't hunt with it. But everyone wants it.

Beads like these could show who you were, who you belonged to... and who owed you. Some researchers think valuables like this were humanity's first store of wealth.

Chapter four. The clay.

Now jump forward to Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq. About five thousand years ago.

People have started farming. There are cities, temples, and huge storehouses full of barley. And now there's a new problem. Who gave what? Who owes what?

So people pressed little marks into wet clay. Sheep. Barley. Oil. Debts.

Here's the amazing part. Some of the oldest writing in the world isn't poetry or prayers. It's accounting. Receipts. I-O-Us.

Money had a unit now. A shekel of silver, about eight grams. But most people never even touched the silver. They ran a tab. The tab was the money.

And just like today, there was interest. Around seventeen fifty B C, King Hammurabi's laws capped interest on loans. Twenty percent on silver. Thirty-three percent on barley.

And when too many farmers fell into debt, some kings did something you'll never see today. They declared a clean slate. Certain debts... simply erased.

Chapter five. Cows, shells and salt.

Around the world, people found their own things to count value with.

Cattle were so important that the Latin word for money, pecunia, comes from pecus... the word for cattle.

In ancient China and across Africa and Asia, people used cowrie shells. Small, shiny, hard to fake. The Chinese character for money and wealth still contains the sign for a shell.

And salt? You've probably heard that Roman soldiers were paid in salt... and that's where the word salary comes from. Historians say there's actually very little evidence for that. Another myth.

Chapter six. The stones of Yap.

Now for the strangest money in the world.

On the tiny Pacific island of Yap, people used giant stone discs as money. They're called rai. Pronounced rye. The biggest is about twelve feet wide and weighs around four tons.

They were carved on another island, Palau, hundreds of miles away, and brought back by canoe.

You can't put a four-ton stone in your pocket. So the stones almost never moved. When one was used as payment, everyone simply remembered that it now had a new owner.

And here's the best part. One giant stone fell into the sea during the trip home. It sank to the bottom. Nobody ever saw it again.

But everyone agreed it was still there. So it kept being used as money. For generations. A fortune that nobody could see... that everyone believed in.

Chapter seven. The first coins.

Coins finally arrive about two thousand six hundred years ago, in the kingdom of Lydia, in modern-day Turkey. Little lumps of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, stamped with a lion.

Why then? Armies. Kings needed to pay soldiers, and soldiers needed something they could carry and spend anywhere.

Coins spread fast. But the old idea of money as a record never went away.

In England, for more than six hundred years, the government kept track of taxes and debts with wooden tally sticks. Notches cut into willow. The stick was split in half. You kept one half. The government kept the other.

In eighteen thirty-four, officials decided to get rid of the old sticks. So they burned them in a furnace under the House of Lords.

The fire got out of control. And it burned down most of the Houses of Parliament.

Chapter eight. Your stone at the bottom of the sea.

So what did ancient humans use before money? Friendship. Gifts. Shells. Clay. Cows. Giant stones. And promises.

Now look at your phone again. Look at that balance.

There's no gold behind it. Most of it isn't even paper. It's a number in someone's computer. A record. A promise that everyone agrees to believe in.

Just like the stone at the bottom of the sea.

The people of Yap would understand your bank account perfectly. Your ancestors in the Kalahari would find it strange. They kept their wealth in people, not numbers.

Maybe that's the oldest money of all.
